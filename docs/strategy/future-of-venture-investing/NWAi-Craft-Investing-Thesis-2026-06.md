# The Craft Investing Thesis

*NWAi Investment Intelligence — strategic thesis, June 2026*
*Author focal point: Jamie (Board Director, Investment Intelligence & AI; TechGroup Co-Chair)*
*Status: thesis draft. Informs future evolution of Screen and Scout stages when ready.*

> **Reader note.** This is an *investing philosophy* doc — about how NWA members form conviction on deals, not about platform architecture or tooling. It does not compete with the Agent-Era Readiness Thesis (which is a deal-assessment lens for evaluating startups). This is about how *we* evaluate deals — the human judgment model at the heart of angel investing, and what AI can and cannot do within it.

---

## The Thesis (One Sentence)

**Angel investing is a craft, not a formula — and the highest-leverage role of AI in our investment process is to surface the signals that let an experienced operator form conviction, not to replace that judgment with a number.**

---

## 1. What Craft Investing Actually Means

David Sacks named his firm Craft Ventures for a reason. The best VCs do not become elite by reading a framework. They become elite by doing hundreds of reps — thousands of founder meetings, product reviews, hiring decisions, company-building cycles. From that accumulation comes something that cannot be encoded in a rubric: **judgment under uncertainty.**

A craft is not pure science. A master carpenter, surgeon, or investor develops:

- Pattern recognition that operates faster than conscious analysis
- Intuition about what's real versus what's narrative
- Taste — the ability to sense when something is genuinely different
- Judgment about which risks are the right risks to take

Early-stage investing has very little data and enormous uncertainty. The best investors — including the best members in this room — are not financial analysts. They are **experienced operators who have seen enough to know when something matters and when it doesn't.** That is the asset NWA has that no algorithm can replicate.

---

## 2. The Problem with Scoring

Numeric scoring systems feel rigorous. They are not. They are precision theater.

When a deal scores 19/30 and another scores 20/30, treating them differently is not analysis — it is a step function masquerading as judgment. Two deals can score identically and be orders of magnitude apart in venture potential. The score averaged away the thing that mattered.

More dangerously: scoring systems reward the *completeness* of a pitch deck, not the *quality* of the opportunity. A polished deck from a late-entrant company beats a rough deck from a pioneer-stage founder who is three years early to a genuinely transformative market — not because the opportunity is better, but because the deck is more complete. That is the opposite of good investing.

The problem is not that scoring is wrong. It is that **scoring as a verdict engine is wrong.** A score that decides is different from a score that informs. NWA's platform should produce the latter and trust its members with the former.

---

## 3. The Member Conviction Model

The question the screening process should actually be optimizing for is not:

*"Does this deal pass our criteria?"*

It is:

*"Which of our members will look at this deal and feel something?"*

Angel investing runs on conviction. Conviction comes from domain knowledge — a member who spent 20 years in manufacturing sees a manufacturing floor control deal and immediately knows whether the problem is real, whether the founder understands the buyer, and whether the solution is the right kind of clever. That pattern recognition is the asset. Our job is to connect it to the right deal at the right moment.

That means the first output of any screening is not a verdict. It is an **orientation** — the signals that tell each member whether this is their kind of deal:

- **What industry and product category is this?** (Not "software" — "software for manufacturing floor control buyers.")
- **Who actually buys it?** The buyer in plain English.
- **What stage is this company at?** Research/Concept, Product, or Business — stated plainly so the member who only invests at the Business stage can self-select out and the member who loves pioneer bets can lean in.
- **Is this company doing something genuinely new, or entering an existing market?** PIONEER / CONTESTED / LATE — a single classification that tells an experienced operator everything about the risk posture.
- **Does this team have the right DNA?** Two fits: can they build it, and do they know who buys it?

These are not scoring dimensions. They are **craft signals** — the information a craftsman needs to engage their expertise.

---

## 4. Screening for the Many vs. the Few

There is a real tension NWA has to manage. At volume — hundreds of inbound pitches per cycle — no amount of craft can be applied deal-by-deal. The throughput problem is real.

The answer is not to automate the judgment. It is to **automate the orientation** and preserve the judgment.

What can be determined deterministically at volume:
- Is the entity US-investable?
- Is the market plausibly venture-scale, or demonstrably capped?
- Is there commercial intent — a product roadmap, a path to market — or is this a pure research project?

These are the triage gates — binary, fast, factual. Everything that clears them deserves an orientation briefing, not a score.

What cannot be determined deterministically at volume — and should not pretend to be:
- Is this genuinely novel?
- Is the founder the right person for this market?
- Does this ride a real structural shift or is it incremental?
- Should NWA make this bet?

These require craft. The orientation briefing creates the conditions for craft to operate. It does not replace it.

---

## 5. Pioneer Bets and the Research/Concept Stage

One implication of the craft model deserves explicit treatment: **genuine structural discontinuity often arrives before the traction signals that most screening systems require.**

A company building the enabling infrastructure for orbital pharmaceutical manufacturing will not have $1M ARR. A founder developing a novel air-film bioreactor that solves a fundamental microgravity bottleneck will not have a commercial customer. A research-stage company in a category that does not yet have a measurable TAM will fail every metric-based screening gate.

And yet these may be exactly the kinds of bets that define a great angel portfolio.

The craft model does not kill pioneer-stage companies at the triage gate. It **surfaces them with honest signals** — Research/Concept stage, pre-revenue, Traction = WEAK — and trusts the member with domain expertise to decide whether the pioneer bet is worth making. A member who understands biotech or space infrastructure or deep physics reads those signals and brings their own judgment to bear. That is the right division of labor.

The worst outcome is a scoring system that kills a genuine pioneer before a craftsman ever sees it.

---

## 6. Weights, Not Gates

Ron Tarro's framing is the right one: **investing is about weights, not gates.**

A single gate for every dimension produces false precision at both ends — it kills deals that should be watched and advances deals that should be declined, because the thing that matters most in *this* deal is not the thing that matters most on average. An experienced investor who looks at a deep-tech pioneer weights novelty and team differently than they weight traction. An investor focused on commercial-stage businesses weights revenue quality and growth trajectory first.

The craft model gives members the weighted mosaic and lets them apply their own weights. It does not apply NWA's average weights on their behalf.

---

## 7. What This Means for NWA's Platform

The implications for how NWA's investment intelligence platform should work:

**Orientation before verdict.** Every deal assessment leads with the signals that help a member decide whether to engage — industry, customer, stage, novelty read, team fit. The verdict follows, informed by a reading of those signals, not derived from a formula applied to them.

**Conviction signals over comprehensive scoring.** The goal of every output is to produce the reaction *"I know something about this market, and I want to dig in"* — or *"this is not my domain, I'll let others lead."* That reaction is worth more than a tidy scorecard.

**Honest signals for pioneer bets.** A Research/Concept company should appear with honest signals, not a clean kill. WEAK traction on a pioneer-stage deal is information, not a verdict.

**Craft questions over verdict questions.** The most valuable output of any early-stage assessment is the question a craftsman would ask — specific, targeted, revealing. Not "does this have a moat?" but "when you describe your advantage over [specific competitor], you lead with distribution — is that a channel or is that an asset?" That is the question that surfaces what a pitch deck never will.

**Member self-identification as the engagement model.** The platform should make it easy for the right member to find the right deal — and to opt out of the wrong ones gracefully. Industry and product category labeling, stage transparency, and buyer-language descriptions are how that happens.
