## The Thesis (One Sentence)

**AI agents are becoming the new substrate through which problems get solved across work, commerce,
and ultimately every domain of economic and social life — and the durability of any startup we
back now depends on whether its founder is solving the problem *as agents will reshape it*, not as
it exists today.**

---

## 1. The Substrate Shift

Start with the one fact that is no longer arguable. In June 2026, Cloudflare reported that **bots
crossed 57.5% of all web traffic** — the first time in the internet's history that machines
out-requested humans. Matthew Prince had forecast the crossover for 2027; it arrived roughly a year
early. Agentic traffic — bots acting *on behalf of* a human — grew nearly **8,000% in a single
year** and now runs at roughly a **1,000:1 ratio**: one human intention spawns a thousand machine
page-loads to satisfy it.

That is the headline. It is also the *least* important part of this thesis.

The web-traffic crossover is not the event. It is the **first visible edge** of a far larger
shift: agents are becoming a general-purpose layer of *agency* in the world — the thing that
actually does the work, makes the request, completes the transaction, runs the process. The
internet is simply where that became measurable first, because HTTP requests are countable.

The deeper claim comes from the people building the substrate, not measuring it:

- **Boris Cherny (Anthropic, creator of Claude Code)** is blunt: coding is, in his words, largely
  *solved*. He has not hand-written a line of code since November; 100% of his code is agent-written;
  he ships 10–30 PRs a day by directing parallel agent fleets. His forecast is not about
  programming — it is about *the firm*: knowledge work broadly automates, the title "software
  engineer" gives way to "builder," and there will be ~100× more people *directing* work than
  *doing* it. The unit of production stops being the human-hour.

- **Andrej Karpathy** supplies the timescale and the posture. He calls this the **decade** — not
  the year — **of agents**, precisely to deflate the hype cycle: working prototypes are here, but
  years of edge-case grind separate a demo from autonomy. His mental model is the **autonomy
  slider** ("build an Iron Man suit, not an Iron Man robot") — graduated automation across *every*
  function, human oversight receding one notch at a time. And his "meet the agents halfway"
  argument — structured content, machine-executable interfaces, an *agent-optimized* world — says
  the environment itself will be rebuilt around non-human actors.

And this is no longer a forecast you have to take on faith — it is arriving from both ends of the
economy at once, top-down and bottom-up.

**Bottom-up — the grassroots proof (OpenClaw).** In late January 2026, an open-source autonomous
agent called **OpenClaw** (Peter Steinberger, ex-PSPDFKit) earned **100,000 GitHub stars in 48
hours**. Treat it not as a product but as a *proxy* for what the substrate shift feels like in the
wild. OpenClaw runs locally, lives inside ordinary messaging apps (WhatsApp, iMessage, Slack), and
*acts* — it browses, transacts, runs commands, and wakes itself on a schedule to monitor the world
on your behalf. The signal is the **human-machine interface flipping**: you stop *operating*
software and start *delegating* to an agent that operates it for you, in plain conversation. The
explosive adoption says the pent-up demand for that flip is already here — agents have left the
research lab for everyday hands.

**Top-down — the boardroom framing (The Economist / Bret Taylor).** In *"How AI agents will
reshape global business,"* The Economist's Andrew Palmer and **Bret Taylor** (Chair of OpenAI,
co-founder of Sierra, ex-co-CEO of Salesforce) describe the same shift remaking the firm. Taylor's
framing is blunt and concrete: *"In 2025, every company needs an AI agent. And the vast majority of
the digital interactions you'll have with your customers will be via your agent. And that agent's
going to do everything your website can do."* The website was the last interface the firm was
forced to build; the agent is the next — and it does not just present information, it *acts*. The
shift remakes work as much as interface: routine and administrative tasks decline while human value
migrates to **oversight, judgment, workflow design, and coordination**, and the agent market is
projected to run from ~$8B in 2025 to **$236B+ by 2034 (>45% CAGR)**. The tell is that The Economist
itself — a 180-year-old publisher — is now building a **two-track internet: one for humans, one for
AI agents.** When the incumbents restructure around the doorway question, the question is settled.

Put the measurer, the builders, the grassroots, and the boardroom together and the thesis writes
itself. **The agent is becoming the predominant actor — and increasingly the predominant
*consumer* — of digital products, information, and transactions. The economy is reorganizing
around a substrate that does the work.**

Two horizons, held at once (this matters for how we score):

- **Near-term (2–3 years, underwritable now):** agents are already the dominant *web* actor,
  already the default coding interface, already collapsing the cost of knowledge work. These are
  facts we can diligence against a company *today*.
- **Decade-out (visionary, directional):** the autonomy slider advances across sales, finance,
  operations, research, commerce, and the physical world. Most current job-shaped problems get
  re-shaped. This is the arc a *visionary* founder is building toward — and the one a fragile
  founder is about to be run over by.

## 2. The Reframe — Agents Reimagine Problems, Not Just Interfaces

Here is the trap, and it is the whole point of this document.

The obvious version of this idea is shallow: *"Is this product built for humans or for bots?"* That
is an interface question. It leads to checklist thinking — does the company have an `llms.txt`, an
API, a markdown mirror. Useful plumbing, weak thesis. It is not what we are testing.

**The real test operates one level deeper: does a future of capable agents *reimagine, dissolve,
or transform the problem the startup is solving* — and is the founder building for that, or for the
world as it is today?**

A worked example. Consider a startup that builds a beautiful comparison-shopping site for a
product category — clean UX, great SEO, affiliate revenue. Excellent solution to the problem *"a
human needs to compare options and decide."* But in the agent era, the human does not browse;
their agent does the comparison, queries structured offers, and transacts. The *problem itself* —
human comparison and decision — **is dissolved**. The site's traffic becomes a thousand bot
page-loads with no ad views, its hosting cost spikes, its affiliate funnel collapses. It was a
great answer to a question agents stop asking.

A founder who *sees* this builds something different: a structured offer-and-transaction layer
agents can call, priced on completed transactions rather than human eyeballs. Same domain.
**Reimagined problem.** That founder is durable. The first one is roadkill with good design.

This is why the screening doorway question — borrowed and sharpened from the source material — is
exactly right *as a doorway*:

> **"Is this information for a human to act on, or a transaction for an agent to complete?"**

For a human, information is an **asset to act on**. For an agent, the same information is a
**transaction to complete**. A startup that has not reckoned with the fact that most of its
"users" may soon be machines — acting for humans, at 1,000× the volume, with none of the
engagement its business model assumes — has a durability problem it probably cannot see.

The doorway question is not abstract — incumbents are already answering it structurally. The
Economist is building a **two-track internet**: one version of its site for humans (designed to be
read, ad- and subscription-supported) and a parallel one for AI agents (structured, queryable, with
its own access and licensing model) — the same content behind two front doors, because the visitor
is now either a person reading or an agent fetching on someone's behalf, and those demand different
formats *and* different business models.

But the doorway is not the building. Walk through it and the real questions are: *Which problems
do agents dissolve? Which do they amplify? Which become newly valuable precisely because agents
exist?* That is what the framework scores.

## 3. Where It Bites First — Two Deep Dives

The substrate shift touches everything eventually. It bites *first*, hardest, and most
diligence-relevant in two domains. These get our depth.

### 3A. Work, the Firm & Economic Structure

This is the Cherny axis, and it is the most underpriced disruption in the current deal flow.

**What changes:**

- **The unit of production flips from human-hour to agent-task.** When one operator directs a fleet
  of agents, the relationship between headcount and output breaks. A 10-person agent-native company
  can deliver what a 1,000-person firm delivered. Capital efficiency goes vertical; the "how many
  engineers / reps / analysts do you need" question changes shape entirely.
- **Business models move from seat → outcome.** Per-seat SaaS pricing assumes a human in every
  seat. When agents do the work, "seats" stop counting anything real. Pricing migrates toward
  outcomes, tasks completed, or compute consumed. **Any startup whose revenue model is per-human-seat
  is exposed at the model level** — not because the product is bad, but because the billing unit is
  evaporating. This is not hypothetical: Bret Taylor's Sierra already runs **outcome-based pricing** —
  it charges only when the agent *resolves* the customer's problem, and escalations to a human are
  free. His framing — *"why pay for a job well done rather than pay for the privilege of using the
  software?"* — is the seat-to-outcome shift showing up in live commercial contracts, not slideware.
  Taylor's deeper point sharpens it: four years ago "software was a productivity tool for a person";
  now the agent *does the job*, and you can measure whether it did.
- **The org chart becomes a manager-of-agents chart.** Headcount-heavy operating plans start to
  read as *liabilities*, not assets. The diligence question "how will you scale the team to support
  this revenue?" inverts: the impressive answer may be "we won't."

**What this means for diligence:**

- A startup *selling into* enterprises must answer: when your customer's workforce is half agents,
  does your product still have a buyer, a budget line, and a seat to sit in?
- A startup *building* in this space must answer: are you selling tools to a shrinking population of
  human "doers," or to the growing population of human "directors" and the agents they run?
- Founder financial literacy now includes **agent-era unit economics**: cost-per-task vs.
  cost-per-seat, margin under agent load, whether their own ops plan reflects agent-native
  efficiency or 2019-era headcount math.

✅ **Durable signals:** outcome/usage pricing; agent-native cost structure; sells to the
director-and-fleet, not the vanishing seat; the product *is* the agent infrastructure.
❌ **Exposed signals:** per-seat revenue model; operating plan that scales headcount linearly with
revenue; value proposition is "makes a human doer 20% faster" at a task agents will own outright.

### 3B. Commerce & Markets

This is the Cloudflare edge, broadened from "web traffic" to "how value gets discovered, priced,
and exchanged."

**What changes:**

- **The buyer becomes an agent.** Discovery, comparison, and purchase increasingly run through a
  human's agent. The marketing funnel built for human attention — ads, SEO, landing pages, brand
  affect — degrades. You are no longer persuading a person; you are being *queried* by a program
  that wants structured facts and the best transaction. And on the *other* side of that query sits
  the company's own agent — Taylor's "every company needs an AI agent." The endpoint is
  **agent-to-agent commerce**: the buyer's agent negotiating with the seller's agent, the
  human-attention middle vanishing from both ends of the transaction at once.
- **Disintermediation of the human-attention middle.** Aggregators, comparison sites, and
  ad-funded discovery layers that profited from *human eyeballs in the middle* get squeezed.
  Agents route around them straight to the transaction. Value re-concentrates at the
  **transactional plumbing** — whoever can actually *complete* the agent's transaction — and at the
  **source of supply**.
- **Machine-to-machine markets and agent-negotiated pricing** emerge: agents transacting with
  agents, price discovery happening at machine speed, "the middleman who gets paid when content is
  consumed by a bot on behalf of a user" becoming a real revenue category.
- **The 1,000:1 cost asymmetry is a P&L event.** Agents load far more than humans without the
  corresponding human monetization. A business that monetizes per human view but serves per agent
  request has a **cost-revenue scissors** opening underneath it.

**What this means for diligence:**

- Where does this company sit when the buyer is a program? At the persuadable-human layer (exposed)
  or at the transaction/supply layer (durable)?
- Does the revenue model survive the shift from human views to agent requests, or does the
  ~1,000:1 load ratio quietly destroy its margins?
- Is there a plan to be *callable* by agents (and monetized on the transaction), or a plan to
  *block* them (defensive, and often a slow bleed of relevance)?

✅ **Durable signals:** captures value at the *transaction*, not the view; positioned as plumbing
or supply, not attention-middle; cost model survives machine-volume traffic; monetizes agent
consumption directly.
❌ **Exposed signals:** ad- or engagement-funded; depends on human funnel persuasion; margin
assumes human-scale traffic; "stay off my lawn" bot-blocking as the entire strategy.

## 4. The Wider Landscape (Supporting)

The same substrate shift ripples outward. We do not score these as deeply, but they are the
analyst's peripheral vision — and for some deals they *are* the center.

- **Personal life & delegation.** Personal agents handle life-admin, scheduling, research,
  purchasing. The "consumer app" competing for a human's time may soon compete for an *agent's*
  selection. Attention as a business model erodes where delegation takes over. OpenClaw is the
  early, concrete instance: a personal agent on your own hardware, reachable in your chat app,
  acting on your behalf — the consumer relationship mediated through the agent, not the screen.
- **Institutions & trust (the dead-internet problem).** With a large and rising share of online
  content AI-generated, **verified-human presence becomes a premium**. Human-verification,
  provenance, and authenticity layers move from nice-to-have to load-bearing. "Prove a human did
  this / wants this / is here" is a real product category.
- **Physical world & embodiment.** The hardest-to-disrupt value is the un-scrapable: real-world
  physical services, high-trust human-to-human interaction, and **intelligent infrastructure** —
  the "digital backbone of the physical world," local compute/inference serving specific
  communities. Atoms remain a moat agents cannot route around.
- **Cognition & the governance of memory.** As agents get better at remembering and connecting
  fragmented data, an inverse market appears: the **architecture of forgetting** — context
  expiration, the right to leave parts of the past behind, governance over what agents retain. A
  durable and very un-obvious frontier. OpenClaw foreshadows the demand: it stores its memory as
  plain files you own, can back up, or can *delete* — local-first **data sovereignty** as a default,
  not an afterthought. The startups that win here treat an agent's memory as something the user
  governs, not something the platform hoards.

## 5. The Four Postures

Every startup we evaluate stands somewhere relative to the substrate shift. Naming the posture is
half the verdict — and it maps cleanly to invest/pass logic.

| Posture | Definition | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Threatened** | Agents reimagine or dissolve the problem this company solves. The solution is excellent for a world that is ending. | ❌ Default toward pass unless a credible path to Riding/Enabling exists. The good design makes this *more* dangerous, not less — it hides the rot. |
| **Riding** | The company *is* the reimagined solution — it solves the problem as agents reshape it. | ✅ The prize. This is where visionary durability lives. Verify it is real, not narrated. |
| **Enabling** | Picks-and-shovels for the agent economy — infrastructure, plumbing, verification, the rails agents run on. | ✅ Often the most underwritable bet of all. Sells into the shift regardless of which application-layer winners emerge. |
| **Insulated** | Genuinely un-disruptable value — physical, high-trust, human-verified, locally embedded. The shift routes around it. | ✅ Durable, but test the claim hard. Most "insulated" companies are actually Threatened and don't know it. |

The single most important discrimination this framework forces: **distinguishing a company that
is *Riding* the shift from one that is *Threatened* by it but tells a Riding story.** A polished
deck about "AI-powered" anything is frequently a Threatened company wearing Riding's clothes.

## 6. What Stays Durable

Pulling the durable signals together — the affirmative side of the thesis, the things that *gain*
value as agents proliferate:

1. **Un-scrapable value** — physical services, high-trust human relationships, anything whose worth
   collapses the moment it is reduced to text an agent can ingest.
2. **Proprietary flywheels that compound under agent load** — data and feedback loops that get
   *richer*, not cheaper, as machine usage scales. (This is where this lens shakes hands with the
   AI Moats and Memory Lock-in frameworks.)
3. **Transactional plumbing** — capturing value at the completed transaction rather than the human
   view; getting paid when an agent acts, not when a person looks.
4. **Intelligent / physical infrastructure** — the backbone the agent economy runs on; local
   compute and inference; the rails, not the ride.
5. **Human-verification & authenticity premiums** — proving humanness and provenance in a
   machine-saturated information ecosystem.
6. **Governance of memory** — context expiration, forgetting, and control over what agents retain.

Bret Taylor sharpens the same point from the incumbent's side. When the marginal cost of *generating*
software falls toward zero — when an agent can write the app — what a customer actually buys from a
vendor is no longer the code. Using an ERP system as his example: *"I'm not sure the cost of writing
software is actually what you're purchasing… you're almost purchasing the audit they've done, the
checks, the bugs other clients found and fixed — that ecosystem of collectively hardening that
system."* **Value migrates from production to trust.** When everyone can generate the artifact, the
moat is the accumulated, hard-won institutional trust the artifact carries — which no agent can
scrape and no competitor can mint overnight.

If a company's durability does not rest on at least one of these in an agent-saturated world, its
moat is probably priced for a world that is ending.

## 7. Implication for NWAi

This becomes our **third always-active investing lens.** The existing two ask:

- **Structural Discontinuity** — is this riding a genuine, irreversible market shift?
- **Memory Lock-in** — does the product get smarter, stickier, more embedded with use?

The third now asks:

- **Agent-Era Readiness** — *is the founder solving the problem as agents will reshape it, and does
  the company's value, model, and moat survive — or strengthen — when agents become the predominant
  actor and consumer?*

It is not a checklist about bots. It is a durability-and-vision test about whether a startup is
built for the world that is arriving. Applied at the doorway with one blunt question — *information
for a human to act on, or a transaction for an agent to complete?* — and resolved with a four-posture
verdict and a scored read on Problem Reimagination, Position in the Agent Economy, and Agent-Era Moat.

The framework that operationalizes this — scoring, signals, the domain-transformation map, and the
posture classification — is specified in

## The Resonating Takeaway

*"For a human, information is an asset to act on. For an agent, it is a transaction to complete. The
internet crossed over to the machines a year ahead of schedule, and the rest of the economy is next.
The question is no longer whether a startup has a good answer — it is whether the question its
product answers will still be asked once the agents arrive. Back the founders building for the
reimagined problem. The ones with the most beautiful solution to a dissolving problem are the most
dangerous bets on the table."*
